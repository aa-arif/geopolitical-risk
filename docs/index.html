Methodology: PITF Structural Model

Track A implements a logistic regression based on the Political Instability Task Force (PITF) model validated in Goldstone et al. (2010). The PITF found that political instability onset can be predicted with over 80% accuracy using just three structural variables: regime type (especially partial democracies with factionalized politics), infant mortality rate (a proxy for state capacity and development), and years since last instability event. These three variables outperformed models with dozens of predictors.

The core logistic model for 30-day instability probability:

logit(P) = -6.5 + 1.8 × is_anocracy (polity score in [-5, +5]) + 1.2 × is_factionalized (polity score in [0, +5]) + 0.5 × ln(infant_mortality) (deaths per 1,000 live births) - 1.0 × stability_factor (min(years_stable, 20) / 20) P(instability) = 1 / (1 + e^(-logit))

The base probability is then modified by three additional components drawn from Fearon & Laitin (2003) and PITF neighborhood contagion research:

P(combined) = P(PITF) × (1 + 0.3 × structural_vulnerability + 0.2 × neighborhood_contagion + 0.1 × GPR_trend)

Structural vulnerability (Fearon-Laitin) combines GDP per capita, population size, terrain ruggedness, and V-Dem liberal democracy index. Neighborhood contagion measures ACLED conflict events in bordering countries over the past 90 days. GPR trend is the Caldara-Iacoviello Geopolitical Risk Index direction for each country.

These probabilities are the outside-view anchor — the structural base rate before any adjustment for current events. In the full system, Track B (LLM forecasting ensemble) adjusts from this anchor using real-time news, ACLED data, and causal reasoning chains. The two tracks are fused with a configurable weight (default 60% Track A / 40% Track B).

References: Goldstone, J. et al. (2010). "A Global Model for Forecasting Political Instability." American Journal of Political Science, 54(1), 190-208. · Fearon, J. & Laitin, D. (2003). "Ethnicity, Insurgency, and Civil War." American Political Science Review, 97(1), 75-90.
